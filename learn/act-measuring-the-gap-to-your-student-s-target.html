ACT: measuring the gap to your student's target

Parents ask about measuring the gap to your student's target more than almost anything else in Scores, usually because the official descriptions are written for educators, not families. Here is the plain-language version: what it is, why it moves scores, and what to do about it at home.

Gap analysis turns a ACT score report into a work plan: take the target, subtract the current score, and then ask the only question that matters — which sections and skills can realistically supply those points in the weeks available. Points are not evenly priced. The cheapest points sit in skills the student almost has — the topic missed by one step, the question type rushed rather than unknown — and a diagnostic sorts skills by exactly that distance. Re-measure after every full practice test and re-rank the gaps; a good plan is rewritten by evidence every few weeks, not defended against it.

Start any work on measuring the gap to your student's target from evidence, not vibes. A short diagnostic set shows exactly which sub-skills are missing, and practice aimed at those gaps moves scores faster than re-doing what your student already knows. GradeHoney's free diagnostic does this mapping automatically, strand by strand.

Progress in measuring the gap to your student's target is easiest to see on a simple tracker: date, set size, score, and the one idea missed most. Four weeks of that log tells a parent more than any single practice score, and it shows the student their own improvement — which is the best motivation there is.

Keep the conversation about measuring the gap to your student's target pointed at process: 'What method did you use?' lands better than 'What did you get?' Process talk builds the habits that scores are made of, and it keeps practice from feeling like a verdict on the student.

A note on materials for measuring the gap to your student's target: the best resource is the one matched to the student's current level, not the hardest one on the shelf. Working one level above comfort — challenging but survivable — is where growth lives; two levels above is where confidence goes to die.

Students remember measuring the gap to your student's target better when they explain it back. Ten minutes of teach-back at the end of a session — the student lecturing the parent, whiteboard optional — converts passive review into durable knowledge more reliably than re-reading ever has. One more thing worth knowing about measuring the gap to your student's target: it responds to feedback faster than to volume. A small set reviewed carefully — every miss explained, every method named — will move measuring the gap to your student's target further in two weeks than a mountain of questions answered and forgotten. Families tell us this page — "ACT: measuring the gap to your student's target" — is the one they bookmark in the Scores library.

If you take one idea from this page, take this: measuring the gap to your student's target is learnable, on a schedule, at home. The families who treat it as a season of steady practice — like music or sport — are the families whose students walk in calm and walk out with a score that reflects their real ability.
